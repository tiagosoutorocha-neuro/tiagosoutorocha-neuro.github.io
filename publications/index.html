  1. CCNEC
    Explainable Machine Learning for Alzheimer’s Disease Detection via Resting-State Electroencephalography
    T. S. Rocha, G. S. M. Gouveia, S. I. A. Oliveira, A. M. Brito, and S. F. Souto
    In 17th Courses and Conferences on Neuroscience and Behavior (CCNEC), Sep 2026
    2nd Place Abs Bib Code Poster

    2nd Place, Best Work Award at the 17th CCNEC, among 80 competing researchers.

    Electroencephalography (EEG) combined with machine learning has high potential for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) screening. However, the clinical adoption of these technologies is hindered by the black-box nature of the algorithms, which classify patients without justifying their criteria. Explainable models are therefore needed to ensure that the model detects real neurophysiological changes rather than signal noise. This study aimed to develop a computational model to distinguish patients with AD from healthy controls and to map, through mathematical interpretability metrics, which EEG spectral signatures guide the classification. Resting-state EEG signals (BrainLat dataset; n = 90) from patients with AD and healthy controls were preprocessed (band-pass filtering, segmentation into 4-s epochs and within-subject normalization). Classic markers from the literature (relative powers, spectral ratios and entropy) fed the classifiers. To avoid bias and data leakage, the model was trained with subject-wise rather than group-wise validation. The most accurate model was dissected with SHAP, which quantifies the weight of each biomarker in the model’s final decision. The optimized classifier achieved a discriminative capacity of 70%. Decoding the model revealed that it detected subjects with AD based on classic cortical slowing: increased relative theta power was the strongest single predictor, followed by the theta/alpha and theta/beta ratios. Notably, the AD probabilities estimated by the model correlated with decline on screening tests (MoCA, p = 0.010). The model distinguished healthy subjects from those with Alzheimer’s disease and autonomously learned the slowing signature of dementia. Explainable AI contributes to the clinical relevance of spectral biomarkers, providing biological reliability to future models that support automated diagnosis.

  3. CCNEC
    Beyond Spectral Power: A Comparative Study of EEG Biomarker Categories Using Machine Learning for Alzheimer’s Disease Screening
    S. I. A. Oliveira, S. F. Souto, T. S. Rocha, G. S. M. Gouveia, and L. M. Paixão
    In 17th Courses and Conferences on Neuroscience and Behavior (CCNEC), Sep 2026
    Abs Bib Code Poster

    Early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease is essential, and electroencephalography (EEG) stands out as a promising, non-invasive and accessible tool. Although the literature reports dozens of biomarkers focused on slowing, connectivity or complexity, most studies only compare classifiers using divergent methodological parameters, so it remains unclear which physiological categories offer the greatest discriminative capacity under identical experimental conditions. This study systematically compared the predictive performance of distinct categories of neurophysiological biomarkers for Alzheimer’s disease screening. Features corresponding to spectral power, slowing (global mean frequency), complexity (Lempel-Ziv), connectivity (phase-amplitude coupling), systemic oscillations (Kuramoto order) and functional disorganization (mutual information) were extracted in five frequency bands from 65 participants (controls and patients with Alzheimer’s disease). The discriminative power of these six categories was tested individually and in a multivariate model with three machine learning algorithms: random forest, support vector machine and extreme gradient boosting. Connectivity (phase-amplitude coupling) and signal complexity (Lempel-Ziv) showed the best individual performance, both reaching 75% precision, whereas classic decline markers such as slowing (mean frequency) and spectral power performed worst, between 56% and 70% accuracy. The multivariate signature integrating all categories outperformed the individual models, reaching 81% precision. Traditionally used biomarkers such as spectral power and slowing are not necessarily the most discriminative when compared under identical methodological conditions with connectivity and complexity biomarkers, reinforcing the central role of network connectivity and nonlinear dynamics in the pathophysiology of Alzheimer’s disease and the importance of analyzing the brain as an integrated system.

  4. CCNEC
    Sleep, Depressive Symptoms, and Cognitive Performance in Older Adults: A Possible Mediation Pathway
    A. M. Brito, T. S. Rocha, J. P. P. Alves, S. F. Souto, and A. N. Santana
    In 17th Courses and Conferences on Neuroscience and Behavior (CCNEC), Sep 2026
    Abs Bib Poster

    Neuropsychiatric symptoms, such as depressive symptoms, and clinical manifestations, such as sleep changes, may emerge in the pre-dementia period, even before cognitive decline becomes evident, suggesting a pathway linking mental health and cognition in older adults. This study assessed whether sleep efficiency and depressive symptoms are associated with cognitive performance in older adults, with pre-specified directional hypotheses that worse sleep and greater severity of depressive symptoms relate to worse cognitive performance. This observational, analytical, cross-sectional study is a secondary analysis of the public RESILIENT dataset (approved by the London-Surrey Borders Research Ethics Committee), comprising 73 older adults with multiple chronic conditions. Participants aged 60 or older with complete data on sleep efficiency, depressive symptoms and cognitive performance were included; cases with missing data on these variables were excluded, resulting in samples of 54 to 69 participants depending on the association tested. Sleep efficiency was assessed with physiological monitoring mattresses, depressive symptoms with the Patient Health Questionnaire, and cognitive performance with the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination. Partial Spearman correlations controlling for age and sex were used. There was no significant correlation between sleep efficiency and cognitive performance (rho = 0.12; p = 0.19; N = 54), possibly due to the small sample size. Depressive symptoms correlated negatively with sleep efficiency (rho = -0.28; p = 0.01; N = 69) and with cognitive performance (rho = -0.33; p = 0.007; N = 54). The absence of a direct association between sleep and cognition, together with the correlations of depressive symptoms, suggests a possible indirect association between sleep and cognition rather than sleep acting in isolation. Depressive symptoms may represent a relevant target for the early prevention of cognitive decline in older adults; longitudinal studies with larger samples are needed to confirm this possible mediation pathway.

  5. CCNEC
    Electroencephalography in the Detection of Alzheimer’s Disease and Mild Cognitive Impairment: An Integrative Review
    J. P. P. Alves, A. M. Brito, T. S. Rocha, S. I. A. Oliveira, and S. F. Souto
    In 17th Courses and Conferences on Neuroscience and Behavior (CCNEC), Sep 2026
    Abs Bib Poster

    Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) call for early and accessible detection strategies. Electroencephalography (EEG) is a promising candidate because it is low-cost, portable and non-invasive, although current methodological heterogeneity limits comparisons. This integrative review aimed to understand how electrophysiological data are processed and analyzed to predict AD and MCI, mapping acquisition parameters, band and connectivity patterns, preprocessing strategies and machine learning models. PubMed and IEEE Xplore were searched with the equation ("EEG" OR "electroencephalography") AND ("Alzheimer" OR "dementia") AND ("MCI") AND ("diagnosis" OR "connectivity"). Peer-reviewed empirical articles in English (2015-2025) comparing controls, MCI and AD in humans were included; simulated data, reviews, protocols and studies without a clear description of the methodology were excluded, for a total of 67 studies. Channels P4, P3 and F7 were the most used, with analyses concentrated on frontal, parietal and occipital regions. Hippocampal atrophy was reflected in temporal slowing and reduced complexity. The theta band was the earliest change, and disease progression was characterized by increased delta and theta, decreased alpha and beta, and reduced fronto-temporal connectivity. Standard preprocessing combined band-pass filtering, notch filtering and ICA to optimize the signal-to-noise ratio. Resting-state protocols with eyes closed predominated (19-32 electrodes, 250-512 Hz, 10-20 system), with screening by MMSE and MoCA. Resting-state, eyes-closed EEG protocols combined with spectral analyses, connectivity metrics and standardized cognitive instruments provide high discrimination between controls, MCI and AD. However, the clinical consolidation of EEG requires rigorous standardization and transparency of protocols.

  7. CogComput
    Understanding Advances in Computational Cognitive Neuroscience Over the Past Ten Years: A Scoping Review
    T. S. Rocha, S. F. Souto, A. M. Brito, T. P. Barbosa, J. P. P. Alves, and 3 more authors
    Cognitive Computation, 2026
    Accepted for publication on September 8, 2026 (in press). Preprint available on PsyArXiv.
    Abs Bib HTML

    Computational Cognitive Neuroscience (CCN) is an interdisciplinary field that uses computational models to understand how the brain gives rise to cognition. Although the field has grown substantially over the past ten years, no review has systematically mapped the major advances achieved during this period. This review therefore aims to map advances in CCN over the past ten years by analyzing the most frequently used computational models, the cognitive domains investigated, and the computational tools employed, and by determining the predominant emphasis of the studies, whether theoretical, translational, or integrated. This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the methodology proposed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and reported following PRISMA-ScR. Searches were performed in PubMed, Embase and IEEE Xplore between 24 and 28 April 2025, and were complemented by a supplementary relevance-ranked Scopus search and by expert-informed identification of additional records. A total of 104 studies were included. Recent progress in CCN was found to be predominantly theoretical, with research focused on memory, learning, language and communication, and decision-making. Recurrent neural networks and Bayesian models emerged as the most widely adopted frameworks. Although CCN has advanced rapidly over the past ten years, the field continues to face critical challenges, notably concerning neurobiological plausibility, model integration, limited external generalizability, and a pressing need for more translational research. Beyond the reviewed corpus, the review discusses emerging AI paradigms, transformers, foundation models, large language models, and neuromorphic computing, as prospective directions informed by complementary literature.
